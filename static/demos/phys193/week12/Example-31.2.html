Example 31.2 · Time Constant of an RL Circuit

Close the switch and the current does not jump: the inductor holds it back, taking the whole battery voltage at first and handing it over to the resistor as the current builds. The hand-over takes a time \(\tau=L/R\). Press play, then try the What If? and the “extra part” with its 212 V.

Problem

Consider the circuit in Figure 31.2 again. Suppose the circuit elements have the following values: \(\varepsilon=12.0\) V, \(R=6.00\ \Omega\), and \(L=30.0\) mH. (A) Find the time constant of the circuit. (B) Switch \(S_2\) is at position \(a\), and switch \(S_1\) is thrown closed at \(t=0\). Calculate the current in the circuit at \(t=2.00\) ms. (C) Compare the potential difference across the resistor with that across the inductor.

Conceptualize

You should understand the operation and behaviour of the circuit in Figure 31.2 from the discussion in this section.

Categorize

We evaluate the results using equations developed in this section, so this example is a substitution problem.

Analyze

  1. (A) Evaluate the time constant from Equation 31.8: \[\tau=\frac{L}{R}=\frac{30.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{H}}{6.00\ \Omega}=5.00\ \text{ms}.\]
  2. (B) Evaluate the current at \(t=2.00\) ms from Equation 31.7: \[i=\frac{\varepsilon}{R}\left(1-e^{-t/\tau}\right)=\frac{12.0\ \text{V}}{6.00\ \Omega}\left(1-e^{-2.00\ \text{ms}/5.00\ \text{ms}}\right)=2.00\ \text{A}\,(1-e^{-0.400})=0.659\ \text{A}.\]
  3. (C) At the instant \(S_1\) is closed, there is no current and therefore no potential difference across the resistor. At this instant the battery voltage appears entirely across the inductor, in the form of a back emf of 12.0 V, as the inductor tries to maintain the zero-current condition. The top end of the inductor is at a higher electric potential than the bottom end. As time passes, the emf across the inductor decreases and the current in the resistor, and hence the voltage across it, increases, as shown in Figure 31.7. The sum of the two voltages at all times is 12.0 V.
What if?In Figure 31.7 the voltages across the resistor and inductor are equal at 3.4 ms. What if you wanted to delay the condition in which the voltages are equal to some later instant, such as \(t=10.0\) ms? Which parameter, \(L\) or \(R\), would require the least adjustment, in terms of a percentage change, to achieve that?

The voltages are equal when the voltage across the inductor has fallen to half its original value, so the time interval required is the half-life \(t_{1/2}\) of the decay, introduced in the What If? of Example 27.10, where \(t_{1/2}=0.693\tau\). For \(t_{1/2}=10.0\) ms we need \(\tau=10.0\ \text{ms}/0.693=14.4\) ms. Holding \(L\) fixed gives \(R=L/\tau=30.0\ \text{mH}/14.4\ \text{ms}=2.08\ \Omega\), a 65 % decrease. Holding \(R\) fixed gives \(L=\tau R=(14.4\ \text{ms})(6.00\ \Omega)=86.4\) mH, a 188 % increase. A much smaller percentage adjustment in \(R\) achieves the desired effect than would an adjustment in \(L\).

Going furtherThe extra part on the publisher’s slides: after a very long time, \(S_2\) is thrown to position \(b\). The right-hand loop now contains an extra resistor \(R_2=100\ \Omega\). At that instant, what are the voltages across \(R\), across \(R_2\), and across the inductor?

The current through an inductor cannot change instantaneously. Just before the switch is thrown it is \(\varepsilon/R=2.00\) A, so just after it is still 2.00 A, now flowing through \(R+R_2=106\ \Omega\): \(\Delta v_R=(2.00\ \text{A})(6.00\ \Omega)=12.0\) V, \(\Delta v_{R_2}=(2.00\ \text{A})(100\ \Omega)=200\) V, and the inductor must supply their sum, \(\Delta v_L=212\) V, many times the battery’s own 12 V. The current then decays with the new, much shorter time constant \(L/(R+R_2)=0.283\) ms. Example 31.3 shows where the energy for this comes from. Use the “throw \(S_2\) to \(b\)” button above to watch it.
