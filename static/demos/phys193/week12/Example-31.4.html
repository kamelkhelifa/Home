Example 31.4 · The Coaxial Cable

No coil anywhere, yet a coaxial cable has inductance. The field between the conductors threads a thin radial slice of the cable, and that slice is one big conducting loop. Add up the flux strip by strip and the inductance falls out of the geometry alone: \(L=\dfrac{\mu_0\ell}{2\pi}\ln\dfrac ba\).

Problem

Coaxial cables are often used to connect electrical devices, such as your video system, and in receiving signals in television cable systems. Model a long coaxial cable as a thin, cylindrical conducting shell of radius \(b\) concentric with a solid cylinder of radius \(a\) as in Figure 31.8. The conductors carry the same current \(i\) in opposite directions. Calculate the inductance \(L\) of a length \(\ell\) of this cable.

InteractiveAdd up the flux through the slice

The book gives no numbers. The starting values are typical of a TV cable: \(a=0.50\) mm, \(b=3.5\) mm, \(\ell=1.0\) m, \(i=1.0\) A. Air between the conductors (\(\mu=\mu_0\)); the thin shell carries no flux of its own.

SolutionStep-by-step solution, as on the slides

Conceptualize

Although we do not have a visible coil in this geometry, imagine a thin, radial slice of the coaxial cable, the light gold rectangle in Figure 31.8. If the inner and outer conductors are connected at the ends of the cable, this slice represents one large conducting loop. The current in the loop sets up a magnetic field between the conductors that passes through this loop. If the current changes, the field changes and the induced emf opposes the original change.

Categorize

We categorize this situation as one in which we must return to the fundamental definition of inductance, Equation 31.2.

Analyze

  1. We must find the magnetic flux through the light gold rectangle. Ampère’s law (§29.3) tells us that the magnetic field in the region between the conductors is due to the inner conductor alone and that its magnitude is \(B=\mu_0i/2\pi r\), where \(r\) is measured from the common centre. Because the field varies with radial position across the rectangle, we must use calculus. Divide the rectangle into strips of width \(dr\) and evaluate the flux through such a strip: \[d\Phi_B=B\,dA=B\ell\,dr.\]
  2. Substitute for the magnetic field and integrate over the entire rectangle: \[\Phi_B=\int_a^b\frac{\mu_0i}{2\pi r}\ell\,dr=\frac{\mu_0i\ell}{2\pi}\int_a^b\frac{dr}{r}=\frac{\mu_0i\ell}{2\pi}\ln\frac ba.\]
  3. Use Equation 31.2 to find the inductance of the cable: \[L=\frac{\Phi_B}{i}=\frac{\mu_0\ell}{2\pi}\ln\frac ba.\]

Finalize

The inductance depends only on geometric factors related to the cable. It increases if \(\ell\) increases, if \(b\) increases, or if \(a\) decreases. This result is consistent with our conceptualization: any of those three changes increases the size of the loop represented by our radial slice, and through which the magnetic field passes, so it increases the inductance. Note also that \(N=1\) here: the whole cable is a single turn, and the inductance comes from the geometry, not from any winding.

Going furtherWhy a logarithm, and why so little inductance?

The field falls as \(1/r\), so each decade of radius contributes the same flux: going from \(a\) to \(10a\) adds exactly as much as going from \(10a\) to \(100a\). That is what \(\ln(b/a)\) says. It also means the inductance is insensitive to the exact dimensions. Making the cable ten times fatter at fixed \(a\) adds only \(\mu_0\ell\ln10/2\pi\approx0.46\ \mu\text{H}\) per metre. Typical values are a few tenths of a microhenry per metre, which is why coaxial cable is used where you want to carry fast signals without much inductance. Slide \(b/a\) over a decade and watch \(L\) grow by that same 0.46 μH/m.

CheckCheck the numbers

The flux integral \(\int_a^b B\ell\,dr\) done numerically (Simpson’s rule), against the closed form; and a second route to the same \(L\) through the energy, \(U_B=\int\frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}dV=\tfrac12Li^2\), integrating the energy density over the cylindrical shells between \(a\) and \(b\).
