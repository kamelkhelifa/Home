Conceptual Example 27.3 · Landscape Lights

Twenty lamps hang in parallel from one long, thin cable. The cable is not a perfect conductor, so every 10-foot stretch of it takes a share of the voltage. Choose the cable and the lamps, and see which of the two candidate answers the circuit picks.

Problem

A homeowner wishes to install low-voltage landscape lighting in his back yard. To save money, he purchases inexpensive 18-gauge cable, which has a relatively high resistance per unit length. This cable consists of two side-by-side wires separated by insulation, like the cord on an appliance. He runs a 200-foot length of this cable from the power supply to the farthest point at which he plans to position a light fixture. He attaches light fixtures across the two wires on the cable at 10-foot intervals so that the light fixtures are in parallel. Because of the cable’s resistance, the brightness of the lightbulbs in the fixtures is not as desired. Which of the following problems does the homeowner have?

(a) All the lightbulbs glow equally less brightly than they would if lower-resistance cable had been used.
(b) The brightness of the lightbulbs decreases as you move farther from the power supply.

InteractiveFollow the voltage down the line

Model and assumptions (the book gives no numbers): copper wire at 20 °C — 12, 14, 16, 18 gauge = 1.588, 2.525, 4.016, 6.385 Ω per 1000 ft of each wire; the current goes out along one wire and back along the other, so each 10-ft stretch of cable adds two wire lengths; every lamp is a fixed resistance \(R_L=V_{\text{rated}}^2/P_{\text{rated}}\) at a rated 12 V.

SolutionThe solution (as on the slides)

A circuit diagram for the system appears in Figure 27.7. The horizontal resistors with letter subscripts (such as \(R_A\)) represent the resistance of the wires in the cable between the light fixtures, and the vertical resistors with number subscripts (such as \(R_1\)) represent the resistance of the light fixtures themselves.

Following the drops down the line

Part of the terminal voltage of the power supply is dropped across resistors \(R_A\) and \(R_B\). Therefore, the voltage across light fixture \(R_1\) is less than the terminal voltage. There is a further voltage drop across resistors \(R_C\) and \(R_D\). Consequently, the voltage across light fixture \(R_2\) is smaller than that across \(R_1\).

Answer

This pattern continues down the line of light fixtures, so the correct choice is (b). Each successive light fixture has a smaller voltage across it and glows less brightly than the one before.

Going furtherWhat would fix it?

Try thicker cable (smaller gauge number), fewer or dimmer lamps, or feeding the cable from its middle. Notice why the first segment matters most: it carries the current of every lamp after it, so its voltage drop is the largest. That is also why the drop per segment shrinks towards the far end.

MethodHow the numbers are computed

The ladder is solved from the far end. Looking right from fixture \(k\), the network is the lamp in parallel with (the next cable segment + everything beyond): \[Z_N=R_L,\qquad Z_k=R_L\parallel\left(R_s+Z_{k+1}\right),\] where \(R_s\) is the resistance of one 10-ft stretch (both wires). Then the voltage divides segment by segment from the supply: \(V_1=V_0\,Z_1/(R_s+Z_1)\), \(V_{k+1}=V_k\,Z_{k+1}/(R_s+Z_{k+1})\). As a check, the currents are recomputed from these voltages and Kirchhoff’s junction rule is tested at every fixture.
