Example 27.1 · Terminal Voltage of a Battery

A real battery is an ideal emf in series with a small internal resistance. Change the load, the emf and the internal resistance, and follow the potential around the loop and the power to where it goes.

Problem

A battery has an emf of 12.0 V and an internal resistance of 0.050 0 Ω. Its terminals are connected to a load resistance of 3.00 Ω. (A) Find the current in the circuit and the terminal voltage of the battery. (B) Calculate the power delivered to the load resistor, the power delivered to the internal resistance of the battery, and the power delivered by the battery.

Conceptualize

Figure 27.1a shows a circuit consistent with the problem statement. The battery delivers energy to the load resistor.

Categorize

This example involves simple calculations from this section, so it is a substitution problem.

Analyze — part (A)

  1. Use Equation 27.3 to find the current in the circuit: \[I=\frac{\varepsilon}{R+r}=\frac{12.0\ \text{V}}{3.00\ \Omega+0.050\,0\ \Omega}=3.93\ \text{A}.\]
  2. Use Equation 27.1 to find the terminal voltage: \[\Delta V=\varepsilon-Ir=12.0\ \text{V}-(3.93\ \text{A})(0.050\,0\ \Omega)=11.8\ \text{V}.\]
  3. To check this result, calculate the voltage across the load resistance \(R\): \[\Delta V=IR=(3.93\ \text{A})(3.00\ \Omega)=11.8\ \text{V}.\]

Analyze — part (B)

  1. Use Equation 26.22 to find the power delivered to the load resistor: \[P_R=I^2R=(3.93\ \text{A})^2(3.00\ \Omega)=46.3\ \text{W}.\]
  2. Find the power delivered to the internal resistance: \[P_r=I^2r=(3.93\ \text{A})^2(0.050\,0\ \Omega)=0.772\ \text{W}.\]
  3. Find the power delivered by the battery by adding these quantities: \[P=P_R+P_r=46.3\ \text{W}+0.772\ \text{W}=47.1\ \text{W}.\]

The live readouts above carry every digit; the steps carry the current rounded to 3.93 A, as on the slides.

What if?As a battery ages, its internal resistance increases. Suppose the internal resistance of this battery rises to 2.00 Ω toward the end of its useful life. How does that alter the battery’s ability to deliver energy?

Connect the same 3.00-Ω load: \(I=\varepsilon/(R+r)=12.0/5.00=2.40\) A, so \(\Delta V=\varepsilon-Ir=12.0-(2.40)(2.00)=7.2\) V — only 60 % of the emf. Then \(P_R=I^2R=17.3\) W and \(P_r=I^2r=11.5\) W: 40 % of the battery’s power now heats the battery itself, compared with 1.6 % when \(r=0.050\,0\ \Omega\). The emf has not changed, yet the battery’s ability to deliver energy to an external load has dropped sharply. Drag \(r\) below and watch both curves.

CheckCheck the numbers

Two independent checks for the present settings: Kirchhoff’s loop rule (the rises and drops around the loop must add to zero) and energy conservation (the power the emf supplies must equal the power dissipated in \(R\) and \(r\)).
