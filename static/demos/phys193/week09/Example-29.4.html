Example 29.4 · Suspending a Wire

Antiparallel currents repel, and each repulsion is \(\mu_0I_1I_2L/2\pi d\). Tilt two of them symmetrically and their sideways parts cancel while their upward parts add; set that against the weight and the current needed follows. Then ask the question the book leaves open: is this balance stable?

Problem

Two infinitely long, parallel wires are lying on the ground a distance \(a=1.00\) cm apart as shown in Figure 29.9a. A third wire, of length \(L=10.0\) m and mass 400 g, carries a current of \(I_1=100\) A and is levitated above the first two wires, at a horizontal position midway between them. The infinitely long wires carry equal currents \(I_2\) in the same direction, but in the direction opposite that in the levitated wire. What current must the infinitely long wires carry so that the three wires form an equilateral triangle?

SolutionStep-by-step solution, as on the slides

Conceptualize

Because the current in the short wire is opposite those in the long wires, the short wire is repelled from both of the others. Imagine the currents in the long wires increased: the repulsive force becomes stronger and the levitated wire rises to a new equilibrium at a higher position. Figure 29.9b shows the desired situation, with the three wires forming an equilateral triangle.

Categorize

Because the levitated wire is subject to forces but does not accelerate, it is modeled as a particle in equilibrium.

Analyze

  1. The horizontal components of the magnetic forces on the levitated wire cancel; the vertical components are both positive and add. Choose the \(z\) axis upward through the top wire, in the plane of the page, and find the total upward magnetic force: \[\vec F_B=2\frac{\mu_0I_1I_2}{2\pi a}L\cos\theta\,\hat{\mathbf k}=\frac{\mu_0I_1I_2L}{\pi a}\cos\theta\,\hat{\mathbf k}.\]
  2. Find the gravitational force on the levitated wire: \(\vec F_g=-mg\,\hat{\mathbf k}\).
  3. Apply the particle in equilibrium model by adding the forces and setting the net force equal to zero: \[\sum\vec F=\vec F_B+\vec F_g=\frac{\mu_0I_1I_2L}{\pi a}\cos\theta\,\hat{\mathbf k}-mg\,\hat{\mathbf k}=0.\]
  4. Solve for the current in the wires on the ground: \[I_2=\frac{mg\pi a}{\mu_0I_1L\cos\theta}.\]
  5. Substitute numerical values, with \(\theta=30.0^\circ\) for the equilateral triangle: \[I_2=\frac{(0.400\ \text{kg})(9.80\ \text{m/s}^2)\pi(0.010\,0\ \text{m})}{(4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \text{T·m/A})(100\ \text{A})(10.0\ \text{m})\cos30.0^\circ}=113\ \text{A}.\]

Finalize

The currents in all wires are on the order of \(10^2\) A. Such large currents would require specialized equipment, so this situation would be difficult to establish in practice.

A question the book leaves openIs the equilibrium of wire 1 stable or unstable?

Raise the wire slightly with the height slider: \(\cos\theta\) grows, but each repulsion, \(\propto1/d\), weakens faster, so the upward force \(\mu_0I_1I_2Lh/\pi(h^2+a^2/4)\) drops below \(mg\) and the wire falls back; lowered, it is pushed up. Vertically the equilateral equilibrium is stable (it sits on the falling side of the force peak at \(h=a/2\); the second crossing, below the peak, is unstable). Sideways a shift toward one lower wire strengthens that push, so the wire slides off: the equilibrium is unstable against horizontal displacement. Tick the box to see the net force for a sideways offset.

CheckCheck the numbers

The book's current from the equilibrium condition; the equilibrium heights found numerically from the force curve; and the stability test, the slope \(dF_{\text{net}}/dh\) at each equilibrium (negative means stable). Horizontal stiffness \(dF_x/dx\) at the equilateral point.
