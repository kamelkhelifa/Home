Example 29.6 · The Magnetic Field Created by a Toroid

Wrap \(N\) turns round a doughnut and every turn pierces a circle drawn inside it. Ampère's law counts them all at once: \(B\cdot2\pi r=\mu_0NI\). The field lives inside the torus, varies as \(1/r\) across it, and a circle drawn outside encloses no net current at all.

Problem

A device called a toroid is often used to create an almost uniform magnetic field in some enclosed area. The device consists of a conducting wire wrapped around a ring (a torus) made of a nonconducting material. For a toroid having \(N\) closely spaced turns of wire, calculate the magnetic field in the region occupied by the torus, a distance \(r\) from the center.

SolutionStep-by-step solution, as on the slides

Conceptualize

Study Figure 29.15 carefully to understand how the wire is wrapped around the torus. Imagine each turn of the wire to be a circular loop as in Example 29.3. The field at the centre of such a loop is perpendicular to the plane of the loop, so the field lines of the collection of loops form circles within the toroid, such as loop 1 in Figure 29.15.

Categorize

Because the toroid has a high degree of symmetry, we categorize this example as an Ampère's law problem.

Analyze

  1. Consider the circular amperian loop (loop 1) of radius \(r\) in the plane of Figure 29.15. By symmetry the magnitude of the field is constant on this circle and tangent to it, so \(\vec B\cdot d\vec s=B\,ds\). The wire passes through the loop \(N\) times, so the total current through it is \(NI\). Apply Ampère's law: \[\oint\vec B\cdot d\vec s=B\oint ds=B(2\pi r)=\mu_0NI.\]
  2. Solve for \(B\): \[B=\frac{\mu_0NI}{2\pi r}.\qquad(29.16)\]

Finalize

This result shows that \(B\) varies as \(1/r\) and hence is nonuniform in the region occupied by the torus. If, however, \(r\) is very large compared with the cross-sectional radius \(a\) of the torus, the field is approximately uniform inside it.

For an ideal toroid the external field is close to zero, but not exactly zero. Imagine loop 1's radius smaller than \(b\) or larger than \(c\): either way it encloses zero net current, so the line integral vanishes. You might think that proves \(B=0\); it does not. Consider amperian loop 2 on the right side of the toroid, whose plane is perpendicular to the page. As charges work their way around the toroid there is a counterclockwise current around it, so a current does pass through loop 2. The toroid therefore acts as a current loop and produces a weak external field. The line integral vanishes for loop 1 because the field lines are perpendicular to \(d\vec s\), not because \(B=0\).

Going furtherHow uniform is "almost uniform"?

Across the cross-section the field goes from \(\mu_0NI/2\pi b\) at the inner wall to \(\mu_0NI/2\pi c\) at the outer wall, a spread of \((c-b)/\bar r\) relative to the middle: a thin ring (\(a\ll r\)) is nearly uniform, a fat one is not. Slide the cross-section radius \(a\) and read the spread. And the weak outside field: the \(N\) turns advance round the ring, so together they carry one effective loop of current \(I\) round the hole, with the field of Example 29.3, \(\sim\mu_0I/2\bar r\) at the centre, \(N\) times smaller than the field inside.
