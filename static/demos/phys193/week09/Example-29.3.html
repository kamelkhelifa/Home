Example 29.3 · Magnetic Field on the Axis of a Circular Current Loop

Each element of the loop sends a tilted \(d\vec B\) to the axis. Its sideways part is cancelled by the element on the opposite side of the ring; its axial part is not. So the field on the axis is the axial part of one element times the circumference, and it falls off as \(1/x^3\) far away, like a dipole.

Problem

Consider a circular wire loop of radius \(a\) located in the \(yz\) plane and carrying a steady current \(I\) as in Figure 29.6. Calculate the magnetic field at an axial point \(P\) a distance \(x\) from the center of the loop.

SolutionStep-by-step solution, as on the slides

Conceptualize

Compare this problem to Example 23.2 for the electric field due to a ring of charge. Figure 29.6 shows the contribution \(d\vec B\) at \(P\) due to a single current element at the top of the ring, resolved into a component \(dB_x\) parallel to the axis and \(dB_\perp\) perpendicular to it. Because of the symmetry, the perpendicular components due to elements at the top and bottom of the ring cancel. This happens for all pairs of segments around the ring, so we can ignore the perpendicular component and focus on the parallel components, which simply add.

Categorize

We are asked to find the magnetic field due to a simple current distribution, so this example is a typical problem for which the Biot–Savart law is appropriate.

Analyze

  1. Every length element \(d\vec s\) is perpendicular to \(\hat{\mathbf r}\) at the location of the element, so \(|d\vec s\times\hat{\mathbf r}|=ds\sin90^\circ=ds\). All length elements are at the same distance \(r\) from \(P\), where \(r^2=a^2+x^2\). Use Equation 29.1: \[dB=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\frac{|d\vec s\times\hat{\mathbf r}|}{r^2}=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\frac{ds}{a^2+x^2}.\]
  2. Find the \(x\) component of the field element, and evaluate \(\cos\theta\) from the geometry: \[(1)\quad dB_x=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\frac{ds}{a^2+x^2}\cos\theta,\qquad(2)\quad\cos\theta=\frac{a}{(a^2+x^2)^{1/2}}.\]
  3. Substitute into Equation (1) and integrate over the entire loop, noting that \(x\) and \(a\) are both constant: \[B_x=\oint\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\frac{ds}{a^2+x^2}\left[\frac{a}{(a^2+x^2)^{1/2}}\right]=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\frac{a}{(a^2+x^2)^{3/2}}\oint ds.\]
  4. The remaining integral is the circumference of the loop, \(2\pi a\): \[B_x=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\frac{a}{(a^2+x^2)^{3/2}}(2\pi a)=\frac{\mu_0Ia^2}{2(a^2+x^2)^{3/2}}.\qquad(29.7)\]

Finalize

To find the magnetic field at the center of the loop, set \(x=0\) in Equation 29.7: \[B=\frac{\mu_0I}{2a}.\qquad(29.8)\] This is exactly the What If? result of Example 29.2. The field-line pattern of a circular current loop is axially symmetric and is similar to the pattern around a bar magnet (Figure 29.7).

What if?What if we consider points on the \(x\) axis very far from the loop? How does the magnetic field behave at these distant points?

In this case, in which \(x\gg a\), we can neglect the term \(a^2\) in the denominator of Equation 29.7: \[B\approx\frac{\mu_0Ia^2}{2x^3}.\] The magnitude of the magnetic moment \(\mu\) of the loop is the product of current and loop area, \(\mu=I(\pi a^2)\) for our circular loop, so \[B\approx\frac{\mu_0}{2\pi}\frac{\mu}{x^3}.\] This is similar in form to the expression for the electric field due to an electric dipole, \(E=k_ep/y^3\). The dashed curve on the plot is this dipole form; the readout shows how far off it is at the chosen \(x\).

CheckCheck the numbers

The Biot–Savart law summed numerically round the loop with the full three-dimensional cross product (no symmetry used): the axial component against Equation 29.7, the perpendicular components, which must sum to zero, and the centre value against Equation 29.8.
