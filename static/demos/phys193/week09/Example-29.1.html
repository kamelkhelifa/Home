Example 29.1 · Magnetic Field Surrounding a Thin, Straight Conductor

Every little piece of the wire is a tiny source: its field at \(P\) is set by \(I\,d\vec s\times\hat{\mathbf r}/r^2\). Walk the element along the wire, watch its contribution rise and fall, and the integral becomes a sum you can see. The answer depends only on the two angles under which \(P\) sees the ends of the wire.

Problem

Consider a thin, straight wire of finite length carrying a constant current \(I\) and placed along the \(x\) axis as shown in Figure 29.4. Determine the magnitude and direction of the magnetic field at point \(P\) due to this current.

SolutionStep-by-step solution, as on the slides

Conceptualize

From the Biot–Savart law, we expect that the magnitude of the field is proportional to the current in the wire and decreases as the distance \(a\) from the wire to point \(P\) increases. We also expect the field to depend on the angles \(\theta_1\) and \(\theta_2\) in Figure 29.4b. We place the origin at \(O\) and let point \(P\) be along the positive \(y\) axis.

Categorize

We are asked to find the magnetic field due to a simple current distribution, so this is a typical problem for the Biot–Savart law. We must find the field contribution from a small element of current and then integrate over the current distribution. The element here is \(I\,d\vec s\), with \(I\) constant and \(d\vec s\) parallel to the current.

Analyze

  1. Consider a length element \(d\vec s\) a distance \(r\) from \(P\). The direction of the field at \(P\) due to the current in this element is out of the page, because \(d\vec s\times\hat{\mathbf r}\) is out of the page. All the current elements lie in the plane of the page, so they all produce a field directed out of the page at \(P\); we need only find the magnitude. Evaluate the cross product: \[d\vec s\times\hat{\mathbf r}=\left|d\vec s\times\hat{\mathbf r}\right|\hat{\mathbf k}=\left[dx\sin\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\theta\right)\right]\hat{\mathbf k}=(dx\cos\theta)\,\hat{\mathbf k}.\]
  2. Substitute into Equation 29.1: \[(1)\quad d\vec B=(dB)\,\hat{\mathbf k}=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\frac{dx\cos\theta}{r^2}\,\hat{\mathbf k}.\]
  3. From the geometry in Figure 29.4a, express \(r\) in terms of \(\theta\): \[(2)\quad r=\frac{a}{\cos\theta}.\] Notice that \(\tan\theta=-x/a\) from the right triangle (the negative sign is necessary because \(d\vec s\) is located at a negative value of \(x\)), so \(x=-a\tan\theta\) and \[(3)\quad dx=-a\sec^2\theta\,d\theta=-\frac{a\,d\theta}{\cos^2\theta}.\]
  4. Substitute Equations (2) and (3) into the expression for the \(z\) component of the field from Equation (1): \[(4)\quad dB=-\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\left(\frac{a\,d\theta}{\cos^2\theta}\right)\left(\frac{\cos^2\theta}{a^2}\right)\cos\theta=-\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi a}\cos\theta\,d\theta.\]
  5. Integrate Equation (4) over all length elements on the wire, where the subtending angles range from \(\theta_1\) to \(\theta_2\) as defined in Figure 29.4b: \[B=-\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi a}\int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2}\cos\theta\,d\theta=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi a}\left(\sin\theta_1-\sin\theta_2\right).\qquad(29.4)\]

Finalize

We can use this result to find the magnitude of the magnetic field of any straight current-carrying wire if we know the geometry and hence the angles \(\theta_1\) and \(\theta_2\). Consider the special case of an infinitely long, straight wire: then \(\theta_1=\pi/2\) and \(\theta_2=-\pi/2\) for length elements ranging between \(x=-\infty\) and \(x=+\infty\), and \[B=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi a}\left[\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\sin\!\left(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)\right]=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi a}(2)=\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi a}.\qquad(29.5)\] Equations 29.4 and 29.5 both show that the magnitude of the magnetic field is proportional to the current and decreases with increasing distance from the wire, as expected. Equation 29.5 has the same mathematical form as the expression for the magnitude of the electric field due to a long charged wire, \(E=\lambda/2\pi\epsilon_0r\).

Going furtherHow long is "infinitely long"?

Stretch the wire with the sliders and watch the running sum approach \(\mu_0I/2\pi a\). A wire ten times longer than the distance \(a\) on each side already gives 99.5 % of the infinite-wire value, because \(\sin\theta\to1\) quickly: the far parts of the wire are both distant and seen at a grazing angle, and contribute almost nothing. The buttons set the book's two special cases: the infinite wire, and a wire that starts at \(O\) (a semi-infinite wire gives exactly half).
