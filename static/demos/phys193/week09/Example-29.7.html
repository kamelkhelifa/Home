Example 29.7 · Magnetic Flux Through a Rectangular Loop

The wire's field is strong near it and weak far away, so the flux through the loop is not "B times A". Cut the loop into strips parallel to the wire: each strip sees one value of \(B\), and adding them gives a logarithm, the signature of a \(1/r\) field.

Problem

A rectangular loop of width \(a\) and length \(b\) is located near a long wire carrying a current \(I\) (Figure 29.21). The distance between the wire and the closest side of the loop is \(c\). The wire is parallel to the long side of the loop. Find the total magnetic flux through the loop due to the current in the wire.

SolutionStep-by-step solution, as on the slides

Conceptualize

As we saw in Figure 29.3, the magnetic field lines due to the wire will be circles, many of which will pass through the rectangular loop. We know that the magnitude of the field is a function of distance \(r\) from a long wire. Therefore the magnetic field varies over the area of the rectangular loop.

Categorize

Because the magnetic field varies over the area of the loop, we must integrate over this area to find the total flux. That identifies this as an analysis problem.

Analyze

  1. Noting that \(\vec B\) is parallel to \(d\vec A\) at any point within the loop, find the flux through the rectangular area using Equation 29.18 and incorporate Equation 29.14 for the field: \[\Phi_B=\int\vec B\cdot d\vec A=\int B\,dA=\int\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi r}\,dA.\]
  2. Express the area element (the tan strip in Figure 29.21) as \(dA=b\,dr\) and substitute: \[\Phi_B=\int\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi r}\,b\,dr=\frac{\mu_0Ib}{2\pi}\int\frac{dr}{r}.\]
  3. Integrate from \(r=c\) to \(r=a+c\): \[\Phi_B=\frac{\mu_0Ib}{2\pi}\int_c^{a+c}\frac{dr}{r}=\frac{\mu_0Ib}{2\pi}\ln r\Big|_c^{a+c}=\frac{\mu_0Ib}{2\pi}\ln\!\left(\frac{a+c}{c}\right)=\frac{\mu_0Ib}{2\pi}\ln\!\left(1+\frac ac\right).\]

Finalize

Notice how the flux depends on the size of the loop. Increasing either \(a\) or \(b\) increases the flux, as expected. If \(c\) becomes large, so that the loop is very far from the wire, the flux approaches zero, also as expected. If \(c\) goes to zero, the flux becomes infinite. In principle this infinite value occurs because the field becomes infinite at \(r=0\), assuming an infinitesimally thin wire. That will not happen in reality, because the thickness of the wire prevents the left edge of the loop from reaching \(r=0\).

Going furtherWhy not just use the field at the middle of the loop?

The naive estimate \(B(c+a/2)\cdot ab\) is compared with the exact flux in the readout. Far from the wire (\(c\gg a\)) the field hardly changes across the loop and the estimate is excellent; close in, the near strips dominate and the logarithm wins: at \(c=a/10\) the naive value is 40 % low. Slide \(c\) toward zero and watch the flux grow without bound while the loop's area stays the same.

CheckCheck the numbers

The flux by two-dimensional numerical integration of \(B\) over the loop (a grid of points, no strip argument), against the logarithm; the naive mid-loop estimate; and the limits \(c\to\infty\) (flux \(\to0\)) and \(c\to0\) (flux \(\to\infty\)).
