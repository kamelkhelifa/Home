Example 22.4 · Find the Charge on the Spheres

Each sphere hangs in equilibrium under three forces: the string tension, gravity and the electric repulsion of the other sphere. The angle of the string measures the electric force, \(F_e=mg\tan\theta\), and Coulomb's law then gives the charge. Run it backwards too: set the charges and let the spheres find their angles.

Problem

Two identical small charged spheres, each having a mass of \(3.00\times10^{-2}\) kg, hang in equilibrium as shown in Figure 22.10a. The length \(L\) of each string is 0.150 m, and the angle \(\theta\) is 5.00°. Find the magnitude of the charge on each sphere.

SolutionStep-by-step solution, as on the slides

Conceptualize

Figure 22.10a helps us conceptualize this example. The two spheres exert repulsive forces on each other. If they are held close to each other and released, they move outward from the center and settle into the configuration in Figure 22.10a after the oscillations have vanished due to air resistance.

Categorize

The key phrase "in equilibrium" helps us model each sphere as a particle in equilibrium. This example is similar to the particle in equilibrium problems in Chapter 5 with the added feature that one of the forces on a sphere is an electric force.

Analyze

  1. The force diagram for the left-hand sphere is shown in Figure 22.10b. The sphere is in equilibrium under the application of the force \(\vec T\) from the string, the electric force \(\vec F_e\) from the other sphere, and the gravitational force \(m\vec g\).
  2. From the particle in equilibrium model, set the net force on the left-hand sphere equal to zero for each component: \[(1)\ \ \sum F_x=T\sin\theta-F_e=0\ \to\ T\sin\theta=F_e,\qquad(2)\ \ \sum F_y=T\cos\theta-mg=0\ \to\ T\cos\theta=mg.\]
  3. Divide Equation (1) by Equation (2) to find \(F_e\): \[\tan\theta=\frac{F_e}{mg}\ \to\ F_e=mg\tan\theta.\qquad(3)\]
  4. Use the geometry of the right triangle in Figure 22.10a to find a relationship between \(a\), \(L\) and \(\theta\): \[\sin\theta=\frac aL\ \to\ a=L\sin\theta.\qquad(4)\]
  5. Solve Coulomb's law (Eq. 22.1) for the charge \(|q|\) on each sphere and substitute from Equations (3) and (4): \[|q|=\sqrt{\frac{F_er^2}{k_e}}=\sqrt{\frac{F_e(2a)^2}{k_e}}=\sqrt{\frac{mg\tan\theta\,(2L\sin\theta)^2}{k_e}}.\]
  6. Substitute numerical values: \[|q|=\sqrt{\frac{(3.00\times10^{-2})(9.80)\tan(5.00°)\,[2(0.150)\sin(5.00°)]^2}{8.988\times10^9}}=4.42\times10^{-8}\ \text{C}.\]

Finalize

If the sign of the charges were not given in Figure 22.10, we could not determine them. In fact, the sign of the charge is not important. The situation is the same whether both spheres are positively charged or negatively charged.

What if?Suppose your roommate proposes solving this problem without the assumption that the charges are of equal magnitude. She claims the symmetry of the problem is destroyed if the charges are not equal, so the strings would make two different angles with the vertical and the problem would be much more complicated. How would you respond?

The symmetry is not destroyed and the angles are not different. Newton's third law requires the magnitudes of the electric forces on the two spheres to be the same, regardless of the equality or nonequality of the charges. The solution to the example remains the same with one change: the value of \(|q|\) in the solution is replaced by \(\sqrt{|q_1q_2|}\) in the new situation. The symmetry of the problem would be destroyed if the masses of the spheres were not the same. In that case, the strings would make different angles with the vertical and the problem would be more complicated.

Switch to Given the charges, find the angles: make the charges unequal and the angles stay equal; make the masses unequal and they do not. (Taking torques about the pivot for the pair, where the electric forces cancel, gives \(m_1\sin\theta_1=m_2\sin\theta_2\).)
