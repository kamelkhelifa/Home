Example 22.5 · A Suspended Water Droplet

A droplet hovers because an upward electric force cancels its weight. With the field pointing down, an upward force needs a negative charge: about thirty thousand extra electrons. Change the field, the mass or the number of electrons and watch the balance tip.

Problem

A water droplet of mass \(3.00\times10^{-12}\) kg is located in the air near the ground during a stormy day. An atmospheric electric field of magnitude \(6.00\times10^3\) N/C points vertically downward in the vicinity of the water droplet. The droplet remains suspended at rest in the air. What is the electric charge on the droplet?

InteractiveBalance the droplet

The drift speed is a model beyond the book: once the forces do not balance, air drag (Stokes' law, \(6\pi\eta rv\), for a droplet of radius \(r\) found from its mass and the density of water) quickly limits the speed to \(v=\Delta F/(6\pi\eta r)\). This is how Millikan's oil-drop experiment measured the charge of single electrons.

SolutionStep-by-step solution, as on the slides

Conceptualize

Imagine the water droplet hovering at rest in the air. This situation is not what is normally observed, so something must be holding the water droplet up.

Categorize

The droplet can be modeled as a particle and is described by two analysis models associated with fields: the particle in a field (gravitational) and the particle in a field (electric). Furthermore, because the droplet is subject to forces but remains at rest, it is also described by the particle in equilibrium model.

Analyze

  1. Write Newton's second law from the particle in equilibrium model in the vertical direction: \[\sum F_y=0\ \to\ F_e-F_g=0.\qquad(1)\]
  2. Using the two particle in a field models mentioned in the Categorize step, substitute for the forces in Equation (1), recognizing that the vertical component of the electric field is negative: \[q(-E)-mg=0.\]
  3. Solve for the charge on the water droplet: \[q=-\frac{mg}{E}.\]
  4. Substitute numerical values: \[q=-\frac{(3.00\times10^{-12}\ \text{kg})(9.80\ \text{m/s}^2)}{6.00\times10^3\ \text{N/C}}=-4.90\times10^{-15}\ \text{C}.\]

Finalize

Noting the smallest unit of free charge in Equation 22.5, the charge on the water droplet is a large number of these units. Notice that the electric force is upward to balance the downward gravitational force. The problem statement claims that the electric field is in the downward direction. Therefore, the charge found above is negative so that the electric force is in the direction opposite to the electric field.

How many electrons?

Dividing by the elementary charge, \(|q|/e=4.90\times10^{-15}/1.60\times10^{-19}\approx3.06\times10^4\): the droplet carries about thirty thousand extra electrons. Charge comes only in whole numbers of \(e\), so the electron count can change only one at a time. Use the extra electrons slider: one electron more or less changes the electric force by about \(1/30\,600\), a few parts in \(10^5\), and the droplet starts to drift very slowly.

CheckCheck the numbers

The book's charge, rounded to a whole number of electrons, used in a numerical integration of the droplet's vertical motion (gravity, electric force and drag) for 10 s from rest: with the balancing charge it should not move.
