Example 30.6 · Electric Field Induced by a Changing Magnetic Field in a Solenoid

A changing magnetic field makes an electric field, even where the magnetic field itself is zero. Outside the solenoid there is no \(\vec B\) at all, yet \(\vec E\) circles the axis and falls off as \(1/r\). Inside it grows as \(r\). The two meet at the wall.

Problem

A long solenoid of radius \(R\) has \(n\) turns of wire per unit length and carries a time-varying current that varies sinusoidally as \(I=I_{\max}\cos\omega t\), where \(I_{\max}\) is the maximum current and \(\omega\) is the angular frequency of the alternating current source (Fig. 30.16). (A) Determine the magnitude of the induced electric field outside the solenoid at a distance \(r>R\) from its long central axis. (B) What is the magnitude of the induced electric field inside the solenoid, a distance \(r\) from its axis?

InteractiveInside and outside

The book gives no numbers. The model uses \(R=2.0\) cm, \(n=1000\) turns/m, \(I_{\max}=5.0\) A and \(f=60\) Hz (\(\omega=2\pi f\)).

SolutionStep-by-step solution, as on the slides

Conceptualize

Figure 30.16 shows the physical situation. As the current in the coil changes, imagine a changing magnetic field at all points in space as well as an induced electric field.

Categorize

Because the current varies in time, the magnetic field is changing, leading to an induced electric field as opposed to the electrostatic electric fields due to stationary electric charges.

Analyze (A), outside the solenoid

  1. First consider an external point and take the path for the line integral to be a circle of radius \(r\) centred on the solenoid as illustrated in Figure 30.16. Evaluate the right side of Equation 30.8, noting that the magnetic field \(\vec B\) inside the solenoid is perpendicular to the circle bounded by the path of integration: \[-\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}=-\frac{d}{dt}(B\pi R^2)=-\pi R^2\frac{dB}{dt}.\qquad(1)\]
  2. Evaluate the magnetic field inside the solenoid from Equation 29.17: \[B=\mu_0nI=\mu_0nI_{\max}\cos\omega t.\qquad(2)\]
  3. Substitute Equation (2) into Equation (1): \[-\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}=-\pi R^2\mu_0nI_{\max}\frac{d}{dt}\cos\omega t=\pi R^2\mu_0nI_{\max}\omega\sin\omega t.\qquad(3)\]
  4. Evaluate the left side of Equation 30.8, noting that the magnitude of \(\vec E\) is constant on the path of integration and \(\vec E\) is tangent to it: \[\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec s=E(2\pi r).\qquad(4)\]
  5. Substitute Equations (3) and (4) into Equation 30.8: \[E(2\pi r)=\pi R^2\mu_0nI_{\max}\omega\sin\omega t.\]
  6. Solve for the magnitude of the electric field: \[E=\frac{\mu_0nI_{\max}\omega R^2}{2r}\sin\omega t\qquad(r>R).\]

Finalize (A)

This result shows that the amplitude of the electric field outside the solenoid falls off as \(1/r\) and varies sinusoidally with time. It is proportional to the current \(I\) as well as to the frequency \(\omega\), consistent with the fact that a larger value of \(\omega\) means more change in magnetic flux per unit time.

Finalize (B)

This result shows that the amplitude of the electric field induced inside the solenoid by the changing magnetic flux through the solenoid increases linearly with \(r\) and varies sinusoidally with time. As with the field outside the solenoid, the field inside is proportional to the current \(I\) and the frequency \(\omega\). The two expressions agree at \(r=R\), where both give \(\mu_0nI_{\max}\omega R\sin\omega t/2\), a useful check on the algebra.

Going furtherWhich way does \(\vec E\) circulate?

Lenz’s law applies to the induced \(\vec E\) too: a current driven by it in a ring around the solenoid would oppose the change of \(B\). While \(B\) (out of the page, say) is decreasing, \(\vec E\) circulates counterclockwise so as to make a field out of the page; while \(B\) is increasing it circulates clockwise. Watch the arrows flip twice per cycle, at the moments when \(\sin\omega t\) changes sign, which are the moments when \(I\) is at a maximum or a minimum and \(B\) is momentarily not changing at all. The field is largest when \(I\) passes through zero.

CheckCheck the numbers

Faraday’s law in integral form, \(\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec s=-d\Phi_B/dt\), tested numerically: the flux through a circle of radius \(r\) is integrated over the area with the actual \(B(r')\) (Simpson’s rule), differentiated in time with central differences, and compared with \(2\pi rE\) from the formulas, for \(r\) inside and outside.
