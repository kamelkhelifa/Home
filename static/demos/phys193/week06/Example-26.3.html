Example 26.3 · The Radial Resistance of a Coaxial Cable

When the area the current passes through changes along the way, \(R=\rho\ell/A\) no longer applies to the whole piece, but it still applies to each thin layer. Slice the plastic into cylindrical shells, add up their resistances, and the logarithm appears. Then test the What If: which way of enlarging the cable leaks more?

Problem

Coaxial cables are used extensively for cable television and other electronic applications. A coaxial cable consists of two concentric cylindrical conductors. The region between the conductors is completely filled with polyethylene plastic as shown in Figure 26.8a. Current leakage through the plastic, in the radial direction, is unwanted. The radius of the inner conductor is \(a=0.500\) cm, the radius of the outer conductor is \(b=1.75\) cm, and the length is \(L=15.0\) cm. The resistivity of the plastic is \(1.0\times10^{13}\ \Omega\cdot\text{m}\). Calculate the radial resistance of the plastic between the two conductors.

SolutionStep-by-step solution, as on the slides

Conceptualize

Imagine two currents as suggested in the text of the problem. The desired current is along the cable, carried within the conductors. The undesired current corresponds to leakage through the plastic, and its direction is radial.

Categorize

Because the resistivity and the geometry of the plastic are known, we find the resistance of the plastic from these parameters. Equation 26.10, however, represents the resistance of a block of material. Because the area through which the charges pass depends on the radial position, we must use integral calculus to determine the answer.

Analyze

  1. Divide the plastic into concentric cylindrical shells of infinitesimal thickness \(dr\) (Fig. 26.8b). Any charge passing from the inner to the outer conductor must move radially through this shell. Use a differential form of Equation 26.10, replacing \(\ell\) with \(dr\): \(dR=\rho\,dr/A\), where \(dR\) is the resistance of a shell of plastic of thickness \(dr\) and surface area \(A\).
  2. Write an expression for the resistance of the hollow cylindrical shell, representing the area as the surface area of the shell: \[dR=\frac{\rho\,dr}{A}=\frac{\rho}{2\pi rL}\,dr.\]
  3. Integrate this expression from \(r=a\) to \(r=b\): \[R=\int dR=\frac{\rho}{2\pi L}\int_a^b\frac{dr}{r}=\frac{\rho}{2\pi L}\ln\!\left(\frac{b}{a}\right).\qquad(1)\]
  4. Substitute the values given: \[R=\frac{1.0\times10^{13}\ \Omega\cdot\text{m}}{2\pi(0.150\ \text{m})}\ln\!\left(\frac{1.75\ \text{cm}}{0.500\ \text{cm}}\right)=1.33\times10^{13}\ \Omega.\]

Finalize

Compare this resistance with that of the inner copper conductor along the same 15.0-cm length, from Equation 26.10: \[R_{\text{Cu}}=\rho\frac{\ell}{A}=(1.7\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\cdot\text{m})\frac{0.150\ \text{m}}{\pi(5.00\times10^{-3}\ \text{m})^2}=3.2\times10^{-5}\ \Omega.\] This resistance is 18 orders of magnitude smaller than the radial resistance. Therefore, almost all the current corresponds to charge moving along the length of the cable, with a very small fraction leaking in the radial direction.

What if?The cable is enlarged to twice the overall diameter, either (1) keeping \(b/a\) fixed or (2) keeping \(b-a\) fixed. Which choice increases the leakage current?

For the current to increase, the resistance must decrease. For choice (1), Equation (1) shows that the resistance is unaffected: it depends on \(a\) and \(b\) only through \(b/a\). For choice (2), charge flows through the same thickness of plastic but through a larger area, so the resistance is lower and the leakage current higher. Try both with the buttons above: (1) gives \(a=1.00\) cm, \(b=3.50\) cm; (2) gives \(a=2.25\) cm, \(b=3.50\) cm.

CheckCheck the numbers

The radial resistance three ways: the exact logarithm of Equation (1), Simpson's rule on \(\int_a^b\rho\,dr/(2\pi rL)\), and the sum of \(N\) thin shells in series (each with its mid-radius area), for increasing \(N\). Then the comparison with the copper core.
