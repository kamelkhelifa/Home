Example 26.1 · Drift Speed in a Copper Wire

Ten amperes through a household wire, and the electrons creep along at a fraction of a millimetre per second. The current is large because there are so many carriers, not because they are fast. Change the current, the wire gauge and the metal, and watch the drift speed.

Problem

The 12-gauge copper wire in a typical residential building has a cross-sectional area of \(3.31\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2\). It carries a constant current of 10.0 A. What is the drift speed of the electrons in the wire? Assume each copper atom contributes one free electron to the current. The density of copper is \(8.92\ \text{g/cm}^3\).

SolutionStep-by-step solution, as on the slides

Conceptualize

Imagine electrons following a zigzag motion such as that in Figure 26.3a, with a drift velocity parallel to the wire superimposed on the motion as in Figure 26.3b. The drift speed is small, and this example helps us quantify the speed.

Categorize

We evaluate the drift speed using Equation 26.4. Because the current is constant, the average current during any time interval is the same as the constant current: \(I_{\text{avg}}=I\).

Analyze

  1. The periodic table shows that the molar mass of copper is \(M=63.5\) g/mol; 1 mol of any substance contains Avogadro's number of atoms, \(N_A=6.02\times10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}\).
  2. Use the molar mass and the density of copper to find the volume of 1 mole of copper: \[V=\frac{M}{\rho}.\]
  3. From the assumption that each copper atom contributes one free electron, find the electron density: \[n=\frac{N_A}{V}=\frac{N_A\rho}{M}.\]
  4. Solve Equation 26.4 for the drift speed and substitute for the electron density: \[v_d=\frac{I_{\text{avg}}}{nqA}=\frac{I}{nqA}=\frac{IM}{qAN_A\rho}.\]
  5. Substitute numerical values: \[v_d=\frac{(10.0\ \text{A})(0.0635\ \text{kg/mol})}{(1.60\times10^{-19}\ \text{C})(3.31\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2)(6.02\times10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1})(8\,920\ \text{kg/m}^3)} =2.23\times10^{-4}\ \text{m/s}.\]

Finalize

Typical drift speeds are very small: at \(2.23\times10^{-4}\) m/s an electron takes about 75 min to travel 1 m. Why, then, does a light turn on almost instantaneously? Changes in the electric field that drives the free electrons travel through the conductor at nearly the speed of light, so electrons already in the bulb start moving within nanoseconds.

What if?If the electrons are so slow, why does the light come on at once?

The wire is already full of free electrons, about \(8.5\times10^{28}\) per cubic metre. Closing the switch sets up the field everywhere along the wire within nanoseconds, so every electron, including those already inside the bulb, starts drifting at the same moment. No electron has to travel from the switch to the bulb. Compare the numbers in the readout: the drift is some 10 orders of magnitude slower than the electrons' random motion (of order \(10^6\) m/s), which carries no net charge at all.

CheckCheck the numbers

An independent route to the same drift speed, through the model of electrical conduction of Section 26.3. With the resistivity \(\rho_e\) of the metal, the field in the wire is \(E=\rho_eJ=\rho_eI/A\) and the average time between collisions is \(\tau=m_e/(nq^2\rho_e)\). A Monte Carlo run follows electrons that are accelerated by the field and lose their direction at random collisions (exponentially distributed free times, mean \(\tau\)); the average velocity it measures is compared with \(I/nqA\).
