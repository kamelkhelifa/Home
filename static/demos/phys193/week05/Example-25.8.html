Example 25.8 · A Partially Filled Capacitor

Slide a thin metal sheet along the face of the dielectric (Example 25.7 says that changes nothing) and the capacitor becomes two in series: one filled with the dielectric, one with air. Change how much of the gap is filled and how strong the dielectric is, and test the two limits.

Problem

A parallel-plate capacitor with a plate separation \(d\) has a capacitance \(C_0\) in the absence of a dielectric. What is the capacitance when a slab of dielectric material of dielectric constant \(\kappa\) and thickness \(fd\) is inserted between the plates (Fig. 25.21a), where \(f\) is a fraction between 0 and 1?

SolutionStep-by-step solution, as on the slides

Conceptualize

In our previous discussions of dielectrics between the plates of a capacitor, the dielectric filled the volume between the plates. In this example, only part of the volume between the plates contains the dielectric material.

Categorize

In Example 25.7, we found that an infinitesimally thin metallic sheet inserted between the plates of a capacitor does not affect the capacitance. Imagine sliding an infinitesimally thin metallic slab along the bottom face of the dielectric shown in Figure 25.21a. We can model this system as a series combination of two capacitors as shown in Figure 25.21b. One capacitor has a plate separation \(fd\) and is filled with a dielectric; the other has a plate separation \((1-f)d\) and has air between its plates.

Analyze

  1. Evaluate the two capacitances in Figure 25.21b from Equation 25.17: \[C_1=\frac{\kappa\epsilon_0A}{fd}\quad\text{and}\quad C_2=\frac{\epsilon_0A}{(1-f)d}.\]
  2. Find the equivalent capacitance \(C\) from Equation 25.12 for two capacitors combined in series: \[\frac1C=\frac1{C_1}+\frac1{C_2}=\frac{fd}{\kappa\epsilon_0A}+\frac{(1-f)d}{\epsilon_0A}=\frac{fd}{\kappa\epsilon_0A}+\frac{\kappa(1-f)d}{\kappa\epsilon_0A}=\frac{f+\kappa(1-f)}{\kappa}\frac{d}{\epsilon_0A}.\]
  3. Invert and substitute for the capacitance without the dielectric, \(C_0=\epsilon_0A/d\): \[C=\frac{\kappa}{f+\kappa(1-f)}\frac{\epsilon_0A}{d}=\frac{\kappa}{f+\kappa(1-f)}C_0.\]

Finalize

Let's test this result for some known limits. If \(f\to0\), the dielectric should disappear. In this limit, \(C\to C_0\), which is consistent with a capacitor with air between the plates. If \(f\to1\), the dielectric fills the volume between the plates. In this limit, \(C\to\kappa C_0\), which is consistent with Equation 25.16.

Going furtherWhy the curve hugs 1 for a large κ.

With strontium titanate (\(\kappa=233\)) filling 90 % of the gap, \(C\) is only about \(9.6\,C_0\), far from \(233\,C_0\): the 10 % of air in series has the smallest capacitance and dominates, just as the smallest capacitor dominates any series combination. Real capacitors with high-κ ceramics are made with the dielectric touching both plates for this reason. And a slab beside the gap instead of across it? If the dielectric filled a fraction \(f\) of the area rather than of the thickness, the two parts would be in parallel and \(C=C_0[1+(\kappa-1)f]\), a straight line. Tick the box to compare.

CheckCheck the numbers

Without the series trick: Gauss's law in the form \(\frac{d}{dy}\!\left(\kappa(y)\epsilon_0\frac{dV}{dy}\right)=0\) is solved across the gap on 500 cells (finite differences with the dielectric constant of each cell, tridiagonal solve) with the plates at \(\Delta V\) and 0. The free charge on the top plate, \(\sigma=-\kappa\epsilon_0\,dV/dy\) at the plate, gives \(C=\sigma A/\Delta V\). The slab is placed at the top, the middle and the bottom of the gap.
