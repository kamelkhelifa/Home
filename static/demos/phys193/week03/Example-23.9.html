Conceptual Example 23.9 · Don’t Use Gauss’s Law Here!

Pick a charge distribution, draw any sphere you like around it, and the computer integrates \(\vec E\cdot d\vec A\) over that sphere. The total always comes out as \(q_{\text{in}}/\epsilon_0\) — Gauss’s law never fails. What fails is the step that would give you \(E\): on these surfaces \(E\) is neither constant nor perpendicular, so it cannot be taken out of the integral.

Problem

Explain why Gauss’s law cannot be used to calculate the electric field near an electric dipole, a charged disk, or a triangle with a point charge at each corner.

InteractiveTry to find a good gaussian surface
  1. Dipole: centre a sphere on it. The flux is zero — and yet the field on the sphere is anything but zero.
  2. Triangle: make the sphere very large. \(E\) becomes nearly constant and radial — from far away three charges look like one. Close in, it does not.
  3. Disk: there is a symmetry axis, but no closed surface on which \(E\) is constant. Compare with the infinite plane of Example 23.8.
SolutionThe answer (as on the slides)

The charge distributions of all these configurations do not have sufficient symmetry to make the use of Gauss’s law practical. We cannot find a closed surface surrounding any of these distributions for which all portions of the surface satisfy one or more of conditions (1) through (4):

  1. the value of \(E\) can be argued by symmetry to be constant over the portion of the surface;
  2. the dot product \(\vec E\cdot d\vec A\) can be expressed as \(E\,dA\) because \(\vec E\) and \(d\vec A\) are parallel;
  3. the dot product is zero because \(\vec E\) and \(d\vec A\) are perpendicular;
  4. the electric field is zero over the portion of the surface.

The distinction to keep

Gauss’s law remains true for every one of these distributions. What fails is not the law but the symmetry — without it the law cannot be solved for \(E\), and the field must be found by integrating Equation 23.1 instead (as this page does to draw the arrows).

MethodHow the numbers are computed

The field anywhere is the vector sum of Coulomb fields (the disk is replaced by 1 152 small charge elements on a polar grid). The flux is integrated over the whole sphere with 48 Gauss–Legendre points in \(\cos\theta\) and 96 equal steps in \(\varphi\). Conditions (1) and (2) are tested on the same 4 608 surface points.
