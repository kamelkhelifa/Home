Example 23.1 · The Electric Field Due to a Charged Rod

A uniformly charged rod is just a very large number of point charges placed end to end. Here you can cut the rod into N pieces, add their fields at P one by one, and watch the sum turn into the integral of the slide.

Problem

A rod of length \(\ell\) has a uniform positive charge per unit length \(\lambda\) and a total charge \(Q\). Calculate the electric field at a point \(P\) that is located along the long axis of the rod and a distance \(a\) from one end.

InteractiveBuild the field from the pieces
  1. Set \(N=1\): the whole rod is treated as one point charge at its centre. How wrong is it when \(P\) is close?
  2. Raise \(N\) and watch the error collapse. The exact answer is what you get when \(N\to\infty\).
  3. Make \(Q\) negative. Every \(dE\) — and so \(\vec E\) — now points towards the rod.
SolutionStep-by-step solution, as on the slides

Conceptualize

The field \(d\vec E\) at \(P\) due to each segment of charge on the rod is in the negative \(x\) direction because every segment carries a positive charge. We expect the field to become weaker as the distance \(a\) becomes larger.

Categorize

The rod is continuous, so this is a continuous charge distribution. Every segment produces a field in the \(-x\) direction, so the contributions can be added as numbers — no vector addition is needed.

Analyze

  1. Put the rod on the \(x\) axis with \(P\) at the origin, from \(x=a\) to \(x=\ell+a\). A segment of length \(dx\) carries \[dq=\lambda\,dx.\]
  2. Magnitude of the field at \(P\) from that segment: \[dE=k_e\frac{dq}{x^2}=k_e\frac{\lambda\,dx}{x^2}.\]
  3. Total field (Equation 23.3): \[E=\int_a^{\ell+a}k_e\lambda\,\frac{dx}{x^2}.\]
  4. \(k_e\) and \(\lambda=Q/\ell\) are constants: \[E=k_e\lambda\left[-\frac1x\right]_a^{\ell+a}.\]
  5. Substitute the limits and \(\lambda=Q/\ell\): \[E=k_e\frac{Q}{\ell}\left(\frac1a-\frac1{\ell+a}\right)=\frac{k_eQ}{a(\ell+a)}.\qquad(1)\]

Finalize

If \(a\) becomes larger the denominator grows and \(E\) becomes smaller, as predicted. If \(a\to0\) (sliding \(P\) to the end of the rod), \(E\to\infty\): \(P\) is then at zero distance from the charge at the end of the rod.

What if?Suppose point \(P\) is very far away from the rod. What is the nature of the electric field at such a point?

For \(a\gg\ell\) the \(\ell\) in the denominator of (1) can be neglected and \(E\approx k_eQ/a^2\): the rod looks like a point charge \(Q\). The plot makes “far” precise: the ratio of the exact field to the point-charge field is \[\frac{E}{k_eQ/a^2}=\frac{a}{a+\ell}=\frac{1}{1+\ell/a},\] so the point-charge formula overestimates the field by the fraction \(\ell/a\). It is within 1 % once \(a>99\,\ell\).
