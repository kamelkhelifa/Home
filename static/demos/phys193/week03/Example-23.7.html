Example 23.7 · A Cylindrically Symmetric Charge Distribution

A cylinder around a line of charge: on the curved side the field is the same everywhere and points straight out; through the two ends nothing passes. That symmetry is the whole solution. In the What If? the line is made finite, and you can watch the symmetry — not Gauss’s law — break.

Problem

Find the electric field a distance \(r\) from a line of positive charge of infinite length and constant charge per unit length \(\lambda\).

InteractiveThe gaussian cylinder
  1. Change \(\ell\). The enclosed charge and the curved area both grow in proportion, so \(E\) does not change. Why must the answer be independent of \(\ell\)?
  2. Double \(r\): the field halves (a point charge’s would quarter).
SolutionStep-by-step solution, as on the slides

Conceptualize & Categorize

The line is infinitely long, so the field is the same at all points equidistant from it, and weakens as we move out radially. The charge distribution has cylindrical symmetry, so Gauss’s law applies.

Analyze

  1. Symmetry requires \(\vec E\) perpendicular to the line and outward. Choose a coaxial cylinder of radius \(r\) and length \(\ell\). On the curved part, \(E\) is constant and perpendicular to the surface (conditions 1 and 2). Through the ends the flux is zero because \(\vec E\) is parallel to them (condition 3).
  2. Gauss’s law with \(q_{\text{in}}=\lambda\ell\): \[\Phi_E=\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec A=E\oint dA=EA=\frac{q_{\text{in}}}{\epsilon_0}=\frac{\lambda\ell}{\epsilon_0}.\]
  3. Curved area \(A=2\pi r\ell\): \[E(2\pi r\ell)=\frac{\lambda\ell}{\epsilon_0}.\]
  4. Solve for \(E\): \[E=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0r}=2k_e\frac{\lambda}{r}.\qquad(23.8)\]

Finalize

The field of a cylindrically symmetric distribution varies as \(1/r\); outside a spherically symmetric one it varies as \(1/r^2\). Equation 23.8 can also be derived by direct integration over the charge distribution.

What if?What if the line segment in this example were not infinitely long?

Then \(E\) is no longer constant over the curved surface (it is weaker near the ends of the line), and it is no longer perpendicular to it (near the ends it has a component along the line). Conditions (1) and (2) fail, so Gauss’s law cannot be solved for \(E\). But Gauss’s law is still true. Below, the exact field of a finite line (by integration, \(E_r=\frac{k_e\lambda}{r}\big[\frac{L/2-z}{\sqrt{r^2+(L/2-z)^2}}+\frac{L/2+z}{\sqrt{r^2+(L/2+z)^2}}\big]\), \(E_z=k_e\lambda\big[\frac{1}{\sqrt{r^2+(z-L/2)^2}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{r^2+(z+L/2)^2}}\big]\)) is integrated over the gaussian cylinder.
