Example 23.8 · A Plane of Charge

For an infinite plane the gaussian “pillbox” can be as long or as short as you like: the flux through it never changes, and so neither does the field. Then put two planes side by side and let superposition do the rest.

Problem

Find the electric field due to an infinite plane of positive charge with uniform surface charge density \(\sigma\).

InteractiveStretch the pillbox
  1. Move the ends of the pillbox further from the plane. Which numbers change? Which do not?
  2. Change the end area \(A\). The enclosed charge and the flux both scale with \(A\) — so \(E\) cannot depend on \(A\).
SolutionStep-by-step solution, as on the slides

Conceptualize & Categorize

The plane is infinitely large, so the field is the same at all points equidistant from it; the distribution is symmetric, so Gauss’s law applies.

Analyze

  1. By symmetry \(\vec E\) is perpendicular to the plane, away from it on both sides. Choose a small cylinder whose axis is perpendicular to the plane, with ends of area \(A\) equidistant from the plane.
  2. \(\vec E\) is parallel to the curved side, so no flux crosses it (condition 3). Through each flat end the flux is \(EA\) (conditions 1 and 2): \[\Phi_E=2EA\quad(\text{zero through the curved surface}).\]
  3. Gauss’s law with \(q_{\text{in}}=\sigma A\): \[\Phi_E=2EA=\frac{q_{\text{in}}}{\epsilon_0}=\frac{\sigma A}{\epsilon_0}.\]
  4. Solve for \(E\): \[E=\frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}.\qquad(23.9)\]

Finalize

The distance from the ends to the plane does not appear in Equation 23.9, so \(E=\sigma/2\epsilon_0\) at any distance: the field is uniform everywhere. It is the same result as Example 23.3 when the disk’s radius becomes infinite.

What if?Two infinite planes of charge are parallel to each other, one positively charged and the other negatively charged, with surface charge densities of the same magnitude. What does the electric field look like?

Each plane alone gives \(\sigma/2\epsilon_0\) on both sides. Between the planes the two fields point the same way and add to \(\sigma/\epsilon_0\); outside they point in opposite directions and cancel. Set the two densities independently below to see the general rule \(E_x=\dfrac{\sigma_1}{2\epsilon_0}\operatorname{sgn}(x-x_1)+\dfrac{\sigma_2}{2\epsilon_0}\operatorname{sgn}(x-x_2)\). Switch on “finite plates” to see why real, finite plates close together give an almost uniform field between them and almost none outside.
