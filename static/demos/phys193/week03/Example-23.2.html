Example 23.2 · The Electric Field of a Uniform Ring of Charge

Every piece of the ring is the same distance from \(P\), and every piece has a partner on the opposite side whose sideways field cancels its own. Rotate the chosen pair around the ring and see the cancellation happen, then release a negative charge near the centre for the What If?.

Problem

A ring of radius \(a\) carries a uniformly distributed positive total charge \(Q\). Calculate the electric field due to the ring at a point \(P\) lying a distance \(x\) from its center along the central axis perpendicular to the plane of the ring.

InteractiveWhy only the axial component survives

  2. Put \(P\) at the centre (\(x=0\)). Every \(d\vec E\) lies in the plane of the ring and they all cancel — is that consistent with the symmetry?
  3. Find the largest field. Does its position depend on \(Q\)? On \(a\)?
SolutionStep-by-step solution, as on the slides

Conceptualize

Resolve the field \(d\vec E\) of one segment into \(dE_x\) along the axis and \(dE_\perp\) perpendicular to it. The segment on the opposite side of the ring produces an equal and opposite \(dE_\perp\). That is true for every pair, so only the parallel components \(dE_x\) remain, and they simply add.

Categorize

The ring is continuous, so this is a continuous charge distribution.

Analyze

  1. Parallel component of the field of a segment \(dq\): \[dE_x=k_e\frac{dq}{r^2}\cos\theta=k_e\frac{dq}{a^2+x^2}\cos\theta.\qquad(1)\]
  2. From the geometry: \[\cos\theta=\frac{x}{r}=\frac{x}{(a^2+x^2)^{1/2}}.\qquad(2)\]
  3. Substitute (2) into (1): \[dE_x=\frac{k_e\,x}{(a^2+x^2)^{3/2}}\,dq.\]
  4. All segments are equidistant from \(P\), so they contribute equally. Integrate over the ring: \[E_x=\int\frac{k_e\,x}{(a^2+x^2)^{3/2}}\,dq=\frac{k_e\,x}{(a^2+x^2)^{3/2}}\int dq.\]
  5. With \(\int dq=Q\): \[E_x=\frac{k_e\,x}{(a^2+x^2)^{3/2}}\,Q.\qquad(3)\]

Finalize

The field points along the axis, away from the ring. It is zero at \(x=0\), consistent with the symmetry, and reduces to \(k_eQ/x^2\) when \(x\gg a\): from far away the ring looks like a point charge. Setting \(dE_x/dx=0\) gives the maximum, at \(x=\pm a/\sqrt2\), where \(E_{\max}=\dfrac{2k_eQ}{3\sqrt3\,a^2}\).

What if?A negative charge is placed at the center of the ring and displaced slightly by a distance \(x\ll a\) along the \(x\) axis. When the charge is released, what type of motion does it exhibit?

For \(x\ll a\), Equation (3) becomes \(E_x\approx k_eQx/a^3\), so the force on a charge \(-q\) is \[F_x=-qE_x=-\frac{k_eqQ}{a^3}\,x .\] That is Hooke’s law with \(k=k_eqQ/a^3\): simple harmonic motion with \(\omega=\sqrt{k_eqQ/(ma^3)}\). Below, the charge’s motion is computed from the exact force (3) step by step (velocity-Verlet) and compared with the SHM prediction. Small release → identical. Large release → the exact motion is slower.

CheckCheck the numbers

Replace the ring by \(N\) equal point charges and add their fields as vectors in three dimensions, at the present \(P\):

Two things to notice. The perpendicular part is zero to rounding error for every \(N\ge2\): opposite pieces cancel exactly. And the axial sum equals Equation (3) for every \(N\), not just in the limit — every piece is the same distance \(r\) from \(P\) and makes the same angle \(\theta\), which is why step 4 of the solution can take \(k_ex/(a^2+x^2)^{3/2}\) outside the integral. (Compare Example 23.1, where the pieces are at different distances and the sum only converges.)
