Example 28.1 · An Electron Moving in a Magnetic Field

The magnetic force is a cross product: it is largest when the velocity and the field are at right angles, zero when they are parallel, and always perpendicular to both. For a negative charge it points the opposite way from \(\vec v\times\vec B\). The force here looks absurdly small, until you divide by the mass of an electron.

Problem

An electron moves through space as a cosmic ray with a speed of \(8.0\times10^6\) m/s along the \(x\) axis (Figure 28.6). At its location, the magnetic field of the Earth has a magnitude of 0.050 mT, and is directed at an angle of 60° to the \(x\) axis, lying in the \(xy\) plane. Calculate the magnetic force on the electron.

SolutionStep-by-step solution, as on the slides

Conceptualize

Recall that the magnetic force on a charged particle is perpendicular to the plane formed by the velocity and magnetic field vectors. Use one of the right-hand rules in Figure 28.5 to convince yourself that the direction of the force on the electron is downward, in the \(-z\) direction in Figure 28.6.

Categorize

We evaluate the magnetic force using the magnetic version of the particle in a field model.

Analyze

  1. Use Equation 28.2 to find the magnitude of the magnetic force: \[F_B=|q|vB\sin\theta=(1.6\times10^{-19}\ \text{C})(8.0\times10^6\ \text{m/s})(5.0\times10^{-5}\ \text{T})(\sin60^\circ)=5.5\times10^{-17}\ \text{N}.\]

Finalize

The vector \(\vec v\times\vec B\) is in the positive \(z\) direction (from the right-hand rule), and the charge is negative, so \(\vec F_B\) is in the negative \(z\) direction. For practice using the vector product, evaluate this force in vector notation using Equation 28.1: with \(\vec v=v\,\hat{\mathbf i}\) and \(\vec B=B\cos60^\circ\,\hat{\mathbf i}+B\sin60^\circ\,\hat{\mathbf j}\), \(\vec v\times\vec B=vB\sin60^\circ\,\hat{\mathbf k}\), and \(\vec F_B=q\vec v\times\vec B=-(5.5\times10^{-17}\ \text{N})\,\hat{\mathbf k}\).

The magnitude of the magnetic force may seem small to you, but remember that it is acting on a very small particle, the electron. To convince yourself that this is a substantial force for an electron, calculate the initial acceleration of the electron due to this force: \(a=F_B/m_e=(5.5\times10^{-17}\ \text{N})/(9.11\times10^{-31}\ \text{kg})=6.1\times10^{13}\ \text{m/s}^2\).

Going furtherA magnetic field can turn a particle; it can never speed one up.

The force is perpendicular to the velocity, so it does no work: \(\vec F_B\cdot\vec v=q(\vec v\times\vec B)\cdot\vec v=0\) identically. The readout shows this dot product and the power delivered, both zero whatever you set. The acceleration, \(6\times10^{13}\) m/s², is enormous, but it only bends the path: the electron starts to circle (or spiral, since \(\vec v\) is not perpendicular to \(\vec B\)) with the radius shown, set by the component of the velocity perpendicular to the field.

CheckCheck the numbers

The cross product computed component by component against \(|q|vB\sin\theta\); the dot products \(\vec F_B\cdot\vec v\) and \(\vec F_B\cdot\vec B\) (both must vanish); the acceleration; and the radius of the resulting circular (or helical) motion, \(r=m v_\perp/|q|B\).
