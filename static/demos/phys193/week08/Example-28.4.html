Example 28.4 · Force on a Semicircular Conductor

Each element of the arc feels \(I\,d\vec s\times\vec B\), but the elements point in all directions, so their forces partly cancel. Add them up and what survives is \(2IRB\), the force on a straight wire spanning the same two points. The straight bottom feels the same, the other way; the loop as a whole feels nothing.

Problem

A wire bent into a semicircle of radius \(R\) forms a closed circuit and carries a current \(I\). The wire lies in the \(xy\) plane, and a uniform magnetic field is directed along the positive \(y\) axis as in Figure 28.20. Find the magnitude and direction of the magnetic force acting on the straight portion of the wire and on the curved portion.

SolutionStep-by-step solution, as on the slides

Conceptualize

Using the right-hand rule for cross products, we see that the force \(\vec F_1\) on the straight portion of the wire is out of the page and the force \(\vec F_2\) on the curved portion is into the page. Is \(\vec F_2\) larger in magnitude than \(\vec F_1\) because the length of the curved portion is longer than that of the straight portion?

Categorize

Because we are dealing with a current-carrying wire in a magnetic field rather than a single charged particle, we must use Equation 28.12 to find the total force on each portion of the wire.

Analyze

  1. Notice that \(d\vec s\) is perpendicular to \(\vec B\) everywhere on the straight portion. Use Equation 28.12 to find the force on this portion: \[\vec F_1=I\int_a^bd\vec s\times\vec B=I\int_{-R}^{R}B\,dx\,\hat{\mathbf k}=2IRB\,\hat{\mathbf k}.\]
  2. To find the magnetic force on the curved part, first write an expression for the force on the element \(d\vec s\) in Figure 28.20: \[(1)\quad d\vec F_2=I\,d\vec s\times\vec B=-IB\sin\theta\,ds\,\hat{\mathbf k}.\] Write \(ds\) from the geometry of the figure: \[(2)\quad ds=R\,d\theta.\]
  3. Substitute Equation (2) into Equation (1) and integrate over the angle \(\theta\) from 0 to \(\pi\): \[\vec F_2=-IRB\int_0^\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta\,\hat{\mathbf k}=-IRB\left[-\cos\theta\right]_0^\pi\hat{\mathbf k}=-IRB(\cos\pi-\cos0)\,\hat{\mathbf k}=-IRB(-1-1)\,\hat{\mathbf k}=-2IRB\,\hat{\mathbf k}.\]

Finalize

Two very important general statements follow from this example. First, the force on the curved portion is the same in magnitude as the force on a straight wire between the same two points. In general, the magnetic force on a curved current-carrying wire in a uniform magnetic field is equal to that on a straight wire connecting the endpoints and carrying the same current. Second, \(\vec F_1+\vec F_2=0\) is also a general result: the net magnetic force acting on any closed current loop in a uniform magnetic field is zero. Zero net force does not mean zero net torque, which is exactly what §28.5 goes on to compute for the same loop.

Going furtherAny shape, same answer.

Because \(\vec B\) is uniform it comes out of the integral: \(\vec F=I\left(\int d\vec s\right)\times\vec B\), and \(\int d\vec s\) is just the straight vector from one end to the other. Switch the shape selector to a triangle or a lumpy arc: the force on the curved part stays \(2IRB\), and the loop's net force stays zero. Rotating the field in the plane changes \(F_1\) and \(F_2\) by the same \(\sin\) factor and they keep cancelling.

CheckCheck the numbers

The force on the chosen curved path summed numerically from \(I\,d\vec s\times\vec B\) over its elements, against \(2IRB\) (times \(\sin\) of the field angle); the force on the straight portion; and their sum.
